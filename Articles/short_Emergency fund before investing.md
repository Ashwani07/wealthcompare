# Emergency Fund First: What Counts as an Emergency, Where to Park It, and Why It Beats Aggressive Investing

Most people know they're supposed to have an emergency fund. Far fewer have actually thought through what an emergency is, where the money should sit, or why this boring, low-return account matters more than the mutual fund SIP they're excited about. This article covers all three, in order.

## What Actually Counts as an Emergency

An emergency is an expense that is **unplanned, urgent, and necessary**, all three. A laptop sale is not one, however good the discount.

**What counts:**

- Job loss or a sharp drop in income (layoffs, or the client who paid most of your bills leaving)
- Medical costs your insurance doesn't cover (co-pays, out-of-network care, an uninsured family member)
- Essential repairs, such as a burst pipe or a vehicle breakdown that stops you earning
- A sudden crisis involving a dependent parent or sibling

**What doesn't, even when it feels urgent:**

- A wedding, festival or holiday you didn't budget for
- A market dip you want to buy into
- Upgrading a phone or car that still works
- Prepaying a loan you can comfortably service

If an expense is predictable, save for it separately. If it's optional, it doesn't belong here. Spend the cushion on something discretionary and it won't be there when a real emergency arrives.

## How Much You Actually Need

The commonly cited range is **3 to 6 months of essential expenses**: rent, groceries, EMIs, insurance premiums, utilities. That means just what you'd need to get by without taking on debt, not your whole lifestyle spend.

- **Stable salary, dual-income household:** around 3 months
- **Freelance, commission-based, or a single income supporting a family:** 6 months, sometimes more
- **Business owner or highly variable income:** 6 to 12 months

Total your real monthly essentials, then multiply by the factor that matches how volatile your income is.

### Worked Example: A ₹60,000/Month Household

First, list what you would still have to pay in a month with no income. Here is an illustrative household whose essentials come to ₹60,000 a month (replace each line with your own):

| Essential expense | Monthly amount |
|---|---|
| Rent or home-loan EMI | ₹20,000 |
| Groceries and household | ₹12,000 |
| Other loan EMIs | ₹8,000 |
| Transport and fuel | ₹5,000 |
| School or tuition fees | ₹5,000 |
| Utilities (electricity, gas, water, internet, mobile) | ₹4,000 |
| Insurance premiums (monthly equivalent) | ₹4,000 |
| Routine medical and medicines | ₹2,000 |
| **Total essentials** | **₹60,000** |

Then multiply that total by the number of months that matches your income risk:

| Months of cover | Emergency fund target | Typical fit |
|---|---|---|
| 3 months | ₹1,80,000 (₹60,000 × 3) | Stable salary, dual-income household |
| 6 months | ₹3,60,000 (₹60,000 × 6) | Single income, or freelance and commission-based work |
| 9 months | ₹5,40,000 (₹60,000 × 9) | Sole earner with dependents and a variable income |
| 12 months | ₹7,20,000 (₹60,000 × 12) | Business owner or highly variable income |

The 12-month row (₹7,20,000) is the corpus used in the worked return example in the Advanced Read.

## Where to Keep the Money

An emergency fund has three jobs, in this order of priority: **liquidity** (you can withdraw within hours to a couple of days), **capital safety** (the value doesn't drop when you need it most), and **some return** (nice to have, never at the cost of the first two).

In practice that usually means a mix of:

- **A savings account** for what you might need instantly, such as bills or a next-day medical expense
- **A sweep-in facility**, if your bank offers one, so idle balances above a threshold earn FD-like returns and stay withdrawable
- **Liquid funds or short-duration debt funds** for the part you're unlikely to need in the next few days

Avoid these for this pool of money:

- **Equity funds or stocks.** Emergencies often coincide with bad markets, exactly when equity is down.
- **FDs with a lock-in or a heavy premature-withdrawal penalty.** Check the exit terms first.
- **Property, gold jewellery, or anything that takes days or weeks to turn into cash.**

**An emergency fund is not an investment. It is insurance you self-fund, in liquid form.** Once you start optimising it for returns, you start treating it like a portfolio, and that is how it ends up in equity just before you need it.

Low risk is not zero risk: a liquid fund can take a credit hit, and bank deposits are insured only up to ₹5 lakh per bank. The Advanced Read at the end covers both.

## Tax and Returns: Don't Let Them Drive the Decision

An emergency fund has one job: the money must be there, in full, when you need it. Returns and tax come a distant second, so don't choose instruments, or time your withdrawals, to optimise either. Treat the interest as ordinary income: report it and pay the tax the law requires.

*A note on section numbers: the Income-tax Act, 2025 replaced the Income-tax Act, 1961 from 1 April 2026. The rules below carried over, but the section numbers changed, so each old number is followed by its new one.*

There is very little to decide anyway. For money you place today, **holding period doesn't change the tax** on a savings account, an FD or a liquid fund, so you never need to time a withdrawal.

- **Savings account interest** is taxed at your slab rate as "Income from Other Sources". Under the old regime, Section 80TTA exempts the first ₹10,000 a year for anyone under 60, and Section 80TTB raises that to ₹50,000 for senior citizens and also covers FD interest (both now sit in Section 153 of the Income-tax Act, 2025). The new regime, now the default, allows neither. Banks don't deduct TDS on savings interest, but you still report it.
- **FD and sweep-in FD interest** is taxed at your slab rate. The bank deducts 10% TDS only once your interest from that bank crosses ₹50,000 in a financial year (₹1,00,000 for senior citizens). Below that, no TDS is cut but the tax is still due, and it accrues every year, not only at maturity.
- **Liquid funds:** for units bought on or after 1 April 2023, gains are taxed at your slab rate under Section 50AA (now Section 76 of the Income-tax Act, 2025), **however long you hold them.**

That is all there is to it: declare the income and pay what's due. If you're still worried about what the fund earns, or how to reduce the tax on it, the **Advanced Read** at the end has a worked ₹7,20,000 example and covers arbitrage funds for higher-slab earners. Most people can skip it.

## The Bottom Line

An emergency fund isn't glamorous, and it won't show up as an impressive number in a portfolio screenshot. Its entire value is negative -  it's the disaster that doesn't happen because you were ready for it. Get the definition of "emergency" right, keep the money boring and liquid, and treat this step as the non-negotiable foundation that comes before any conversation about aggressive investing, not a parallel track to it.

### Why Emergency Savings Come Before Aggressive Investing

Starting a SIP feels productive, and holding cash feels like doing nothing. There are three reasons the cash comes first:

- **You avoid selling investments at the worst time.** Without a buffer, a job loss or medical bill forces you to sell equity, often in a downturn, since financial stress and market stress tend to arrive together. That locks in a loss you didn't need to take.
- **You avoid high-interest debt.** Otherwise the emergency lands on a personal loan or credit card at rates far above what your investments earn, and you cannot out-invest a high-interest debt trap. If you're deciding between prepaying a loan and investing instead, the [Loan vs SIP calculator](/tools/loan-vs-sip/) models that trade-off.
- **You can stay invested.** A funded reserve is what lets you leave your equity alone during a correction. Investors without one tend to panic-sell.

Sequencing matters. An aggressive SIP with zero buffer is underinsured risk-taking, not discipline. Build the 3–6 month buffer first, then direct fresh money toward long-term investing. If you're doing both on a limited surplus, ask which allocation reduces your risk of a forced, expensive decision later.

### Can Your Capital Actually Fall? Being Honest About Risk

None of these instruments behave like equity, but "low risk" is not "zero risk".

- **Liquid funds** hold money-market instruments maturing within about 91 days, which keeps interest-rate risk minimal. They still carry **credit risk**: if a company whose paper the fund holds defaults or is downgraded, the NAV can take a sudden markdown. This has happened in India during the credit events of 2018–2020, when some debt schemes wrote down part of their NAV or side-pocketed exposure to a defaulting issuer. High-quality liquid funds from large fund houses that stick to top-rated paper reduce this risk but don't eliminate it. Read the portfolio holdings before parking a large sum.
- **Bank deposits** carry the least market risk but do carry counterparty risk. The Deposit Insurance and Credit Guarantee Corporation (DICGC) covers only up to **₹5 lakh per depositor per bank**, principal and interest combined. Above that, the excess is at risk if the bank fails, which is rare, though India has had cooperative bank failures within the last decade. Splitting a large fund across two or three well-rated banks is sensible, not paranoid.

A plain savings account is closer to zero risk than a liquid fund or an arbitrage fund (covered below). That gap is the trade-off you accept for a somewhat better return, and it is acceptable as long as you don't stake the whole fund on a single scheme.

### Should You Use a Separate Bank Account for This?

Yes. The biggest threat to an emergency fund is rarely a market crash. It's you, three months from now, facing a "too good to skip" sale with a debit card linked to the same account. A separate account, ideally at a different bank from your daily spending, does two things a mental note never will:

- **Adds friction.** An extra login or an extra day makes you more likely to reconsider.
- **Removes ambiguity.** Emergency money mixed into your main balance makes you look richer than you are, so a separate account keeps your "spendable" number honest.

Fund redemptions already add a small speed bump, since they need a deliberate app transaction. The savings-account portion is the one most worth isolating, because it has no friction otherwise.

### Is 12 Months Overkill? An Honest Take

Conventional guidance is 3–6 months, and 12 is the far edge of that range, not the middle. Whether it fits depends on the shape of your income, not on how safe you want to feel.

**Twelve months is defensible if** your income is genuinely variable, you're the sole earner supporting dependents, or your industry has long re-employment gaps for your role.

**It's excess caution if** you have a stable salary (especially with a second income in the household) and health insurance that covers most medical shocks, yet you're holding 12 months at 4–6% post-tax while delaying equity investing that has historically compounded at meaningfully higher real rates.

Every month of buffer beyond your real risk profile is money that isn't compounding over your investing horizon. Holding 12 months when 6 covers you is a choice to prioritise psychological safety over capital efficiency. That is a rational trade-off if you make it deliberately, and not if it's just inertia. The fix isn't to shrink the fund reflexively: total your essentials today, be honest about how variable your income is, and set the multiple from that math.

### What the Fund Earns After Tax

Take the ₹60,000/month household from the earlier table, on the new tax regime (the default for most people now), holding the 12-month fund: ₹7,20,000 split as 2 months in savings, 4 months in a sweep-in FD and 6 months in a liquid fund. The illustrative annual rates are 3% (savings) and 6.5% (FD and liquid fund). Rates vary by bank and fund, so check current numbers before relying on this.

**Step 1: gross annual return.** Each bucket earns its amount × its rate, and the gross return is the sum of the three:

| Bucket | Months of expenses | Amount | Assumed annual rate | Gross annual return |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Savings account | 2 | ₹1,20,000 | 3% | ₹3,600 |
| Sweep-in FD | 4 | ₹2,40,000 | 6.5% | ₹15,600 |
| Liquid fund | 6 | ₹3,60,000 | 6.5% | ₹23,400 |
| **Total** | **12** | **₹7,20,000** | **5.92% blended** | **₹42,600** |

**Step 2: tax and net return.** Tax due = gross return × slab rate. Net return = gross return − tax due. Effective post-tax rate = net return ÷ ₹7,20,000:

| Slab | Gross annual return | Tax due | Net return | Effective post-tax rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 15% | ₹42,600 | ₹6,390 | ₹36,210 | 5.03% |
| 20% | ₹42,600 | ₹8,520 | ₹34,080 | 4.73% |
| 30% | ₹42,600 | ₹12,780 | ₹29,820 | 4.14% |

These figures use simple interest for one year on the full amount, with no compounding or withdrawals. They also leave out the 4% health and education cess, which raises each tax figure by 4% (at the 30% slab, ₹12,780 becomes ₹13,291). The table starts at 15% because, under the new regime, taxable income up to ₹12 lakh is effectively tax-free through the Section 87A rebate, so 15% is the lowest rate that realistically applies to this interest.

Since FDs and liquid funds are taxed the same way, choose between them on liquidity and convenience alone.

### Using Arbitrage Funds to Cut the Tax

This suits higher-slab earners (especially at 30%) who want to cut the tax on an emergency fund without adding real market risk.

**What an arbitrage fund is.** It holds 65%+ of its portfolio in equities and sells matching futures against those positions, locking in the price gap between the cash and futures markets instead of betting on direction. Its return depends on that spread, which tends to track short-term interest rates, so it behaves much like a debt fund even though it is legally built from equity and equity derivatives.

**Why that matters for tax.** Above the 65% equity threshold it counts as an **equity-oriented fund** for tax, a label based on portfolio composition rather than risk. That mismatch is what helps here:

- Redeemed within 12 months: STCG at a **flat 20%**, whatever your slab. At 30% that already beats a liquid fund.
- Redeemed after 12 months: LTCG at **12.5%, with gains up to ₹1.25 lakh per financial year exempt.** For an emergency-fund-sized gain, that can cover the whole thing.

The catch is that the ₹1.25 lakh exemption is one pooled limit across **all** your equity and equity-fund long-term gains for the year, not a separate allowance per fund. If you've booked LTCG elsewhere, the remainder is taxed at 12.5%. Check what you've already realised before assuming zero tax.

**The comparison.** Take the ₹3,60,000 "least likely to be touched soon" portion of a 12-month fund, assumed to earn an illustrative 6% a year (verify current fund returns):

| Scenario | Tax treatment | Tax at 15% slab | Tax at 20% slab | Tax at 30% slab |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Liquid fund, any holding period | Slab rate | ₹3,240 | ₹4,320 | ₹6,480 |
| Arbitrage fund, redeemed within 12 months | Flat 20% STCG | ₹4,320 | ₹4,320 | ₹4,320 |
| Arbitrage fund, redeemed after 12 months | 12.5% LTCG above ₹1.25L pooled exemption | ₹0* | ₹0* | ₹0* |

*Assuming no other equity LTCG has already used up that year's exemption.

At the 15% and 20% slabs, the gap between liquid and arbitrage funds is small (at most ₹4,320 on this amount), so tax shouldn't be the deciding factor. At 30%, arbitrage funds pull meaningfully ahead, which is why they're recommended to higher earners, not because the instrument is objectively better money.

**Risk and friction.** The hedge can show brief mark-to-market mismatches in market stress. The documented case is the COVID crash of March 2020, when the hedge briefly produced mildly negative monthly returns before the spread normalised at futures expiry. Redemption also takes about a day (T+1) rather than being instant, and many arbitrage funds charge an exit load, often around 0.25%–0.5%, if redeemed within roughly the first 15–30 days. If an emergency hits within days of investing, that load is a real cost.

**The verdict.** Arbitrage funds are a legitimate tool for the higher-slab portion of an emergency fund, but they add moving parts (a hedge that can wobble, an exit-load window, and a shared LTCG exemption) for a tax advantage that mostly matters at 30%. If you won't track those, the simpler liquid-fund-and-FD setup isn't worse, only less tax-optimised.
