The 4% Rule Fails in India: Sustainable SWP Rates with 6% Inflation

Published 30 October 2026 · 9 min read · Regulatory and mathematical reference for Indian retirees

For thirty years, personal finance blogs and FIRE (Financial Independence, Retire Early) forums have repeated a simple axiom: multiply your annual living expenses by 25, save that amount, and withdraw 4% in year one adjusted for inflation thereafter. You will never run out of money.

This rule originates from financial planner William Bengen's landmark 1994 study, which tested historical US stock and bond returns over 30-year rolling retirement horizons. However, blindly applying Bengen's 4% rule to an Indian retirement portfolio is one of the most dangerous mistakes an Indian household can make.

In India, where lifestyle inflation compounds at 6% to 7% and real post-tax returns are narrow, a 4% initial systematic withdrawal rate (SWP) carries an alarming risk of depleting a retirement corpus within 18 to 22 years. Here is the mathematical reality of sustainable retirement withdrawals in India.

1. Why the US 4% Rule Breaks Down in India

Bengen’s original model succeeded because the economic backdrop in the United States enjoyed unique conditions that do not apply to India:

  • Persistent Inflation: Historical US inflation averaged ~2.5% to 3.0% over Bengen’s test periods. In India, long-term consumer inflation (CPI) hovers around 5.5%–6.0%, while urban middle-class lifestyle inflation (healthcare, education, technology, utilities) routinely runs at 7.0% to 8.5%.
  • Thin Real Returns: A conservative post-retirement Indian asset allocation (30% equity, 70% debt and arbitrage) might generate a gross return of ~8.0% per annum. After subtracting direct fund expense ratios (0.5%) and capital gains taxes on withdrawals (~1.0% effective return drag), the net portfolio return is roughly 6.5% to 7.0%. Against 6.0% inflation, your net real return is just 0.5% to 1.0%.
  • Longer Indian Retirement Horizons: Many modern urban Indians aspire to retire early at age 50 or 55. With modern healthcare pushing life expectancies to 85–90 years, a retirement in India must survive for 35 to 40 years, not the 30 years assumed in the 4% rule.

2. The Math: 4% vs 3% Initial SWP on a ₹3 Crore Corpus

Consider a retiree at age 60 who enters retirement with an accumulated post-tax corpus of ₹3,00,00,000 (₹3 Crore). Assume an average net post-retirement portfolio return of 7.0% per annum and lifestyle inflation of 6.0% per annum.

Let us compare two strategies: Path A (the Western 4% rule = ₹12,00,000 initial annual withdrawal) versus Path B (a calibrated 3% initial SWP = ₹9,00,000 initial annual withdrawal), both escalating withdrawals each year by 6% inflation.

Under Path A (4% initial withdrawal), the portfolio appears healthy for the first 10 years because initial nominal interest outpaces withdrawals. But as inflation doubles the annual expense from ₹12 Lakh to ₹27 Lakh by Year 15, the withdrawal exceeds annual growth, initiating a rapid death spiral. The 4% portfolio completely runs out of cash by Year 23 (at age 83).

Under Path B (3% initial withdrawal), the corpus retains sufficient capital buffer during the first 15 years to easily withstand 30 full years of withdrawals, leaving over ₹1.28 Crore at age 90.

3. The Hidden Killer: Sequence of Returns Risk

The mathematical schedule above assumes an unrealistically smooth 7.0% return every single year. In reality, market returns are volatile.

If you retire into a major economic recession—such as the 2008 Global Financial Crisis or the 2020 crash—and your equity bucket drops 25% to 35% in Year 1 or Year 2, withdrawing a fixed inflation-adjusted rupee amount forces you to sell equity units at depressed prices. Those sold units can never participate in the subsequent market recovery.

This phenomenon is called Sequence of Returns Risk. A severe market downturn during the first 3 to 5 years of retirement can cut the lifespan of a portfolio in half, even if the 30-year average return matches your initial projection.

4. The Sustainable Indian Framework: The 3-Bucket System

To insulate an Indian retirement from sequence risk and high inflation, financial planners rely on the Three-Bucket Strategy:

Bucket Time Horizon Recommended Asset Class Primary Objective
Bucket 1: Cash & Liquidity Years 1 to 3 High-yield savings sweep FDs, liquid mutual funds, arbitrage funds 100% capital safety and immediate liquidity. SWP is disbursed from here. Zero equity risk.
Bucket 2: Income & Accrual Years 4 to 8 Target Maturity Debt Funds, Banking & PSU Debt, Senior Citizens Savings Scheme (SCSS), RBI Floating Rate Bonds Predictable yield, steady capital preservation. Replenishes Bucket 1 every 2 to 3 years.
Bucket 3: Growth & Inflation Hedge Years 9 to 30+ Direct Nifty 50 Index Funds, Flexicap funds, balanced advantage funds Long-term compounding to beat 6% lifestyle inflation. Never touched during market crashes.

5. Rules for Running a Tax-Efficient SWP in India

  1. Use Arbitrage Funds for Short-Term Cashflows: Under Indian tax laws, arbitrage mutual funds are taxed as equity (Section 196/198: 20% STCG for <1 year, 12.5% LTCG above ₹1.25L for >1 year). For retirees in the 30% tax slab, arbitrage funds offer vastly superior post-tax yields compared to bank FD interest.
  2. Harvest the Annual LTCG Exemption: Section 198 of the Income-tax Act allows an annual tax-free equity capital gains threshold of ₹1,25,000 per financial year. Systematically redeeming and re-entering equity units each year resets your acquisition cost base.
  3. Adopt Dynamic Withdrawals: If the equity market crashes by over 15% in a calendar year, pause inflation escalations for non-essential lifestyle spending (vacations, luxury upgrades) for 12 months. This simple behavioral guardrail adds 4 to 6 years of corpus longevity.
