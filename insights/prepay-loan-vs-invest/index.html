When you have surplus cash (an annual bonus, an inheritance, or spare monthly cash flow), you face one of personal finance's classic dilemmas: should you prepay your home loan or invest that surplus in an equity mutual fund SIP?

One side argues that prepaying saves interest and delivers complete peace of mind. The other points to historical stock market returns of 12% to 14% and argues that paying down an 8% loan leaves money on the table.

Both arguments contain truth, but neither tells the whole story. Here is the mathematical comparison, incorporating taxes, inflation, and sequence-of-returns risk.

The core trade-off: Certainty vs expected return

Every rupee used to prepay an 8% loan generates an immediate, guaranteed, risk-free post-tax return of 8%. There are no market crashes, no fund manager mistakes, and no capital gains taxes to deduct from that saving.

Conversely, investing that rupee in equity mutual funds carries an expected return, but not a guaranteed one. Furthermore, mutual fund profits are subject to Long-Term Capital Gains (LTCG) tax (12.5% on gains exceeding ₹1.25 lakh under Section 198 of the Income-tax Act, 2025) and ongoing fund management fees.

What the numbers tell us

  • On paper, investing generates more wealth: If equity delivers a steady 12% gross return, investing the surplus builds ₹1.23 crore after tax, compared to ₹82.89 lakh in Path B. That is an expected nominal wealth edge of roughly ₹40 lakh (or ₹12.57 lakh in today's purchasing power).
  • Prepayment creates immense peace of mind: Under Path B, you become completely debt-free more than 5.5 years earlier (at month 173 instead of month 240), eliminating 67 months of loan risk.
  • Prepay + Reduce EMI performs surprisingly well: By reducing your contractual EMI and investing the difference every single month, you achieve ₹1.01 crore in terminal wealth while lowering monthly financial pressure immediately.

The hurdle rate formula

To decide whether investing makes mathematical sense, you must calculate your hurdle rate: the minimum return your investments must deliver after tax and expenses to match the guaranteed savings of prepaying.

Hurdle Gross Return = [ Loan Interest Rate ÷ (1 − Capital Gains Tax Rate) ] + Fund Expense Ratio

For an 8.5% home loan rate, 12.5% LTCG tax, and a 0.5% fund expense ratio:

Hurdle Return = [ 8.5% ÷ (1 − 0.125) ] + 0.5% = 9.71% + 0.5% = 10.21%

If your diversified portfolio cannot achieve at least 10.21% compounded annually over the holding period, prepaying the loan wins on pure arithmetic. Any return above 10.21% is your net reward for bearing market risk.

The hidden risk of the investment path

Spreadsheet models assume a smooth 12% return every single year. Real equity markets do not work that way. Consider two scenarios:

  • Bull market early, bear market late: If a market crash occurs in Year 18 of your 20-year loan, your SIP corpus can drop by 25% to 35% right before your target date. Meanwhile, your loan interest was compounding relentlessly.
  • Job or income shock: A home loan EMI is a mandatory legal contract. If household income is interrupted, you cannot tell the bank to pause your EMI. If your surplus went into prepayments, your loan balance (and potential EMI burden) is lower. If your surplus was invested in equity during a bear market, redeeming units at distress prices locks in permanent capital loss.

A practical decision framework

Rather than viewing this as an all-or-nothing choice, match the decision to your life stage and circumstances:

When prepaying the loan makes the most sense:

  1. Early in the tenure: In years 1 through 5, when 70% to 80% of every EMI is interest. Prepaying here yields exponential interest savings over time. See How Your EMI Splits Between Interest and Principal.
  2. Interest rates are elevated: If home loan rates rise above 9% or 9.5%, the hurdle rate exceeds 11%, making prepayments compelling.
  3. Job or industry volatility: If your career involves commission-heavy pay, startup equity, or cyclical hiring risks, becoming debt-free shields your family from insolvency.

When investing the surplus makes the most sense:

  1. Late in the loan tenure: If you only have 4 or 5 years remaining, most of your EMI is already principal repayment. Prepaying late saves relatively little interest.
  2. Long investment horizon (15+ years): Equity compounding over 15+ years has historically beaten loan interest rates in India by a comfortable margin.
  3. Adequate liquidity buffer: You already maintain a robust emergency reserve of 6 to 12 months' expenses in safe bank FDs and liquid funds. See Emergency Fund Before Investing.

The 50/50 balanced approach

Many experienced financial planners recommend splitting your surplus: put 50% toward loan principal prepayment and invest the remaining 50% into an equity SIP. This delivers the emotional satisfaction of watching your debt shrink while keeping your compounding engine running in the capital markets.

Advanced Read: Methodology & Assumptions

Loan amortization mechanics

Monthly loan interest is computed as Balance × (Interest Rate ÷ 1200). Prepayments made during a month reduce closing balance immediately, lowering interest in all subsequent months.

Tax computation on investment corpus

Under Section 198 of the Income-tax Act, 2025 (formerly Section 112A of the 1961 Act), LTCG on equity mutual fund units held over 12 months is taxed at 12.5% on gains exceeding ₹1,25,000 per financial year. Tax is applied once at terminal redemption in this comparison.

Discounting to today's rupees

Nominal future wealth figures are deflated using Real Wealth = Nominal Wealth ÷ (1 + inflation)^years. This puts both paths into equivalent purchasing power.
