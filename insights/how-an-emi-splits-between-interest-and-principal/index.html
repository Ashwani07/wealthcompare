How Your EMI Splits Between Interest and Principal

Published 12 October 2026 · 6 min read (plus an optional 1 min Advanced Read)

You take a home loan, pay your EMI for a year, and then check how much you owe. The balance has barely moved. It feels wrong, and many people suspect the bank has made a mistake. It hasn't. This is how a loan works, and once you see it month by month, a lot of loan advice starts to make sense.

Below is one loan, followed month by month and year by year. The 8.5% rate is only an example. The arithmetic is the same at any rate.

One Loan, One Month at a Time

Say you borrow ₹40,00,000 at 8.5% a year for 20 years. Your EMI is ₹34,713. Here is where each payment goes at six points along the way.

In the first month, ₹28,333 of your ₹34,713 pays the lender interest. Only ₹6,380 reduces what you owe. By the last payment the picture has flipped: almost all of it repays the loan.

Why Interest Comes First

The lender charges interest on the amount you still owe. At the start you owe all ₹40 lakh, so one month of interest is ₹40,00,000 × 8.5% ÷ 12 = ₹28,333. Your EMI never changes, so whatever is left after interest, ₹6,380, goes to the loan.

Next month you owe a little less, so the interest is a little less, and a little more of the same EMI goes to the loan. This repeats every month. The interest part shrinks slowly. The principal part grows slowly. Because the loan is so large and the principal part is so small at the start, it takes years for the two to change places.

In the first year you pay ₹4,16,555 in EMIs. ₹3,36,946 of it, about 81%, is interest. Your balance falls from ₹40,00,000 to ₹39,20,391, only ₹79,609 lower.

After five years you have paid ₹20.83 lakh in EMIs. You still owe ₹35,25,087, which is 88% of the loan. You have repaid only ₹4.75 lakh. After ten years you have paid ₹41.66 lakh and still owe 70%.

When the Loan Turns

For this loan, more of each EMI goes to interest than to the loan until EMI number 142, which is 11 years 10 months in. From EMI 143 onwards the loan gets the bigger share.

Half of the loan is repaid only after 13 years 10 months, which is 69% of the way through the tenure. You are well past the middle of the loan before you are halfway out of debt.

Over the whole 20 years you repay ₹83,31,103. Of that, ₹43,31,103 is interest, so for every ₹100 you borrowed you pay about ₹108 in interest.

A Longer Tenure Makes It Worse

A longer tenure lowers the EMI, which is why lenders offer it. It also keeps the balance high for longer, so a bigger share of the early payments is interest.

Moving from 15 years to 25 years cuts the EMI by ₹7,180 a month, but adds ₹25.73 lakh of interest. It takes 16 years 10 months before the loan gets the bigger share, against 6 years 10 months.

What This Means for You

  • Early prepayments do the most work. A lump sum in year 1 removes principal when almost all of every EMI is interest, so it stops a long run of future interest. The same amount paid in year 15 saves far less. Our article on reducing the EMI or the tenure after a prepayment puts numbers on it.
  • Your balance is higher than you expect. If you plan to sell the property, move the loan or close it early, use the real balance and not what you think you have paid off. Your lender can give you the exact figure. In the early years it is close to the original loan.
  • A small rate difference matters most at the start. One extra percentage point is charged on the biggest balance. Compare rates before you sign. The EMI Calculator shows the effect of one point on both the EMI and the total interest.
  • Look at the schedule, not only the EMI. Two loans with similar EMIs can cost very different amounts in total. Ask for the repayment schedule and read the interest column.

To see your own loan split this way, put its amount, rate and tenure into the EMI Calculator. It shows the same year-by-year split and the month when your loan turns.

What the Example Leaves Out

The example uses one fixed rate for 20 years. Many home loans are floating, so the rate follows a benchmark and can change. When it does, either your EMI or your tenure moves, and the split changes with it. When a lender resets rates for floating-rate borrowers, the Reserve Bank of India's FAQs say it should offer a higher EMI or more EMIs (or a mix), a switch to a fixed rate where the lender offers one, and the option to prepay in part or in full.

It also leaves out processing fees, insurance, late charges and tax benefits on the interest. Your lender's own schedule can differ by small amounts because of rounding and how it counts days. The pattern, with interest first and principal later, is the same for every reducing-balance loan.

The Bottom Line

An EMI is not split evenly. In the early years most of it is interest, because interest is charged on a balance that has barely fallen. Over this ₹40 lakh loan, interest is 81% of the first year's payments and the loan turns only after 11 years 10 months. That is why early prepayments save more, longer tenures cost more, and the balance falls more slowly than you expect.

The Two Lines of Maths

Let B be the balance before a payment and r the monthly rate (the yearly rate divided by 12 and by 100). Each month, interest = B × r, and principal repaid = EMI − interest. The new balance is B minus the principal repaid. With the EMI = P × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)−n) for a loan of P over n months, the balance reaches exactly zero after the last payment.

After k payments the balance has a closed form: P × (1 + r)k − EMI × ((1 + r)k − 1) ÷ r. For the example, after 60 payments this gives ₹35,25,087, which matches the table.

Why Principal Grows Every Month

The principal part of each EMI is the previous month's principal part multiplied by (1 + r). It grows by the monthly rate, here 8.5% ÷ 12 = 0.71% a month. So the principal part of the 60th payment is ₹9,675, and of the 120th is ₹14,777. The first was ₹6,380. Growth of about 0.7% a month sounds small, but it compounds over 240 months, which is why the last payments are almost entirely principal.

Finding the Turning Point

Principal is larger than interest once the principal part is more than half the EMI. Since the principal part in month t is p1 × (1 + r)t−1, we need t − 1 to be more than ln(EMI ÷ (2 × p1)) ÷ ln(1 + r). Here p1 = ₹6,380 and EMI ÷ 2 = ₹17,356, so t is above 142.8. The first whole month that qualifies is month 143, which matches the schedule.
