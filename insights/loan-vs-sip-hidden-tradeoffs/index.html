Loan vs SIP: The Hidden Trade-offs Most Comparisons Miss

Why "EMI vs SIP amount" isn't the comparison you think it is

Published August 2026 · WealthCompare Insights

Ask around, and you'll hear the same rough comparison for "should I buy a house or invest instead": look at the EMI, look at what a SIP of the same amount could grow to, and see which number is bigger. It's simple, it's intuitive, and it quietly ignores at least five things that materially change the answer.

The comparison most people actually make

It usually goes something like this: "My EMI would be ₹70,000 a month. If I invested ₹70,000 a month in a SIP instead at 12% for 20 years, I'd have a much bigger number than the flat is worth." And then a decision gets made on that basis.

The problem isn't the math — the compounding calculation itself is fine. The problem is what's missing from the comparison:

  • The down payment, which the property buyer pays but the "just invest instead" comparison usually forgets to invest too.
  • Inflation, which erodes both numbers, but isn't always applied consistently to both sides.
  • Tax — capital gains tax applies to the SIP corpus when you eventually sell, but this rarely makes it into the back-of-envelope version.
  • Real transaction costs — stamp duty and registration on the property side, expense ratios on the SIP side.
  • What each option actually gives you beyond a number — a place to live versus a liquid, flexible asset.

None of these are exotic. They're just tedious to track by hand, which is exactly why they get skipped — and why the skipped version can point you toward the wrong decision.

Trade-off 1: Nominal value isn't real value

A property "worth ₹2.65 crore in 20 years" and a SIP "worth ₹8.8 crore in 20 years" are both nominal numbers — actual future rupees, not adjusted for the fact that a rupee in 2046 buys less than a rupee today. At 6% average inflation, ₹100 today is worth roughly ₹31 in today's terms after 20 years. Comparing two nominal numbers without deflating both by the same inflation assumption isn't comparing wealth — it's comparing two different currencies and pretending they're the same one.

The fix is straightforward once you know to do it: divide every future value by (1 + inflation)^years before comparing anything. It changes headline numbers a lot, and it changes them for both sides — which is exactly why skipping it doesn't just shrink the numbers, it can shift the winner.

Trade-off 2: Your down payment isn't free money

Here's the trade-off that gets missed most often. If you buy a ₹1 crore property with an ₹80 lakh loan, you're putting ₹20 lakh of your own cash in on day one. If the "alternative" is just investing your EMI every month, that ₹20 lakh has vanished from the comparison — it's sitting in the property in one scenario and nowhere in the other.

A fair comparison invests that ₹20 lakh too — as a lump sum, on day one, in the SIP scenario. Otherwise you're not comparing "buy vs. invest," you're comparing "commit ₹20 lakh cash and an EMI" against "commit only an EMI," which was never the actual choice on the table.

Trade-off 3: Tax treatment isn't symmetric

Long-term capital gains on equity mutual funds are currently taxed at 12.5% on gains above a ₹1,25,000 annual exemption. That tax bill shows up once, when you actually redeem — not every year you hold. A comparison that ignores it overstates what the SIP path actually hands you; a comparison that applies it every year overstates the drag, since you're not selling annually. Either mistake pushes the "winner" in the wrong direction.

Property has its own tax dimension too — capital gains on sale, though most people hold a primary residence long enough, and with enough exemptions available, that this rarely bites the way SIP taxation does. The asymmetry is real, and it's worth knowing which way it cuts for your specific numbers rather than assuming.

Trade-off 4: The costs nobody puts in the spreadsheet

Both paths have real costs that a quick mental comparison tends to leave out entirely:

Property sideTypical cost
Stamp duty3–7% of property value (varies by state)
Registration~1%
Brokerage1–2%, if applicable
Ongoing maintenanceSociety charges, repairs, property tax
SIP sideTypical cost
Expense ratio~0.5–1.5% per year (drag on returns, usually already reflected in a fund's stated CAGR)
Exit loadUsually nil after 1 year, but check the specific fund

Stamp duty and registration alone can be 4–8% of the property price, paid once, upfront, in cash — on top of the down payment. Skip it in your mental model and you're comparing an artificially cheap property against a full-cost SIP.

Trade-off 5: A number isn't the whole decision

This one won't show up in any calculator, including ours, and it deserves saying plainly: a house gives you somewhere to live, stability against rent increases, and control over your space. A SIP gives you liquidity — you can access it in a bad year without selling your home. Neither of these is a rupee figure, and neither should be pretended away by whichever number happens to be bigger. If the financial gap between the two paths is small, the non-financial factors are probably what should actually decide it.

A worked example

Take a fairly typical scenario: a ₹1 crore property, ₹80 lakh loan at 8% over 20 years, 1% annual maintenance, 5% property appreciation, 6% inflation, and a SIP at 12% expected return investing the same down payment and EMI.

Property, real value after 20 years (net of interest & maintenance) ₹18,58,137
SIP, real value after 20 years (after tax) ₹2,47,12,629
Difference, in today's purchasing power ₹2,28,54,492 in favor of SIP

That's a wide gap, and it's specific to these assumptions — a lower SIP return, higher property appreciation, or a different tenure changes it meaningfully, sometimes enough to flip which side wins. That sensitivity is exactly why a single generic answer ("SIP always wins" or "property always wins") is the wrong takeaway — the honest one is "it depends on your numbers, and here's how to check yours."

So which one wins?

It depends on assumptions you actually control the inputs to: your expected property appreciation (which varies enormously by city and even by neighborhood), your conviction in long-term equity returns, your tax bracket, and how many years you're comparing over. As a rough pattern: SIP tends to look stronger when property appreciation is modest relative to inflation, when the tenure is long enough for compounding to dominate, and when equity return assumptions hold up. Property tends to close the gap — sometimes reverse it — in high-appreciation markets, shorter tenures, or when the "cost" of renting instead is factored in on the other side (a comparison this article hasn't made, since renting is a different decision than investing).

None of this replaces running your own numbers, which is the entire point of the next section.
