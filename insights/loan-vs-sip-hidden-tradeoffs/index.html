Loan vs SIP: The Hidden Trade-offs Most Comparisons Miss

Updated 4 October 2026 · 8 min read (plus an optional 2 min Advanced Read)

A plain-language guide to comparing a home loan with a SIP, and the five things a quick comparison leaves out.

What this article is about: Many people compare a home loan with a SIP by putting the EMI next to the SIP amount and seeing which number is bigger. That shortcut leaves out five things, and any one of them can change the answer. This article explains the five in simple words, then shows you how to check your own numbers with our free calculator.

Who it is for: anyone choosing between buying a property with a home loan and investing the same money in a SIP. Not for: someone who needs a home to live in and is choosing between renting and buying. That is a different question, and a Rent vs Buy calculator is on the way.

The Quick Comparison, and Why It Misleads

Here is how most people do it:

"My EMI would be about ₹1.04 lakh a month. If I put ₹1.04 lakh a month into a SIP at 12% for 20 years, I'd have roughly ₹10 crore. The flat won't be worth that. So the SIP wins."

The maths in that sentence is fine. The problem is what it leaves out:

  • Your down payment. The property side uses your cash on day one. The SIP side forgets to invest it.
  • Inflation. Prices rise, so future rupees buy less than today's rupees.
  • Tax. Each side is taxed in a different way.
  • Extra costs. Stamp duty, maintenance and fund charges.
  • Things money can't count. A place of your own, and cash you can reach quickly.

None of these is hard to understand. They are just boring to track by hand, so people skip them.

1. Future Rupees Are Worth Less Than Today's Rupees

Prices go up every year. So a rupee in 2046 buys less than a rupee today. At 6% inflation, ₹100 today buys only about ₹31 worth of things after 20 years.

That is why "₹4 crore in 20 years" sounds bigger than it really is. To compare fairly, convert both sides into today's money using the same inflation rate. The result is called the real value: what the money is worth in today's rupees. If you skip this step, the numbers don't just shrink. The winner can change.

2. Your Down Payment Is Also Money You Could Invest

Say you buy a ₹1.6 crore flat with a ₹40 lakh down payment and a ₹1.2 crore loan. On day one, ₹40 lakh of your own cash goes into the flat.

If the "invest instead" side only counts the monthly EMI, that ₹40 lakh vanishes from the comparison. A fair comparison invests the ₹40 lakh too, as a lump sum on day one. Otherwise the SIP side starts with less money than the property side.

(In India, banks can lend only up to 75% of the value on loans above ₹75 lakh, so for a flat this size at least 25% has to come from you.)

3. Tax Is Different on Each Side

SIP: you pay capital gains tax once, when you sell. For equity funds held over a year, it is 12.5% on the profit above ₹1.25 lakh in that year. You don't pay it every year you hold, so a comparison that charges tax every year makes the SIP look worse than it is. A comparison that ignores tax makes it look better.

Property: you also pay tax when you sell. If you held the property for more than two years, it is 12.5% on the profit, with no inflation adjustment for property bought after 23 July 2024. You can cut or remove this tax by putting the profit into another house (Section 82 of the Income-tax Act, 2025, formerly Section 54). So the tax disappears only if you buy another home.

Home-loan tax deductions can also matter. Our example leaves them out to keep things simple, so check what applies to you.

4. Costs Nobody Puts in the Spreadsheet

Both paths have costs that a quick comparison forgets.

What you pay Typical size
Property Stamp duty Roughly 6% to 7% of the price in Pune and Pimpri-Chinchwad, depending on the buyer. Check the current rate with the state's registration department
Property Registration fee 1%, capped at ₹30,000
Property Broker fee Often 1% to 2%, if you use one
Property Maintenance, repairs, property tax Every year
SIP Fund charges Taken from the fund's returns automatically. SEBI sets a limit on what a fund can charge
SIP Exit load A fee if you sell early, often within the first year. Check the fund

On a ₹1.6 crore flat in Pune, stamp duty and registration come to roughly ₹9.9 lakh to ₹11.5 lakh. You pay that in cash, once, on top of your down payment. Leave it out and the property looks cheaper than it really is.

5. Some Things Aren't Numbers

A property can give you a place to live or rent to earn. A SIP gives you money you can reach in a bad year without selling your home. Both matter, and no calculator can put a rupee value on them.

So use the numbers first. If the money gap between the two paths is small, these personal factors should decide. If the gap is huge, ask yourself honestly whether the comfort is worth that much.

A Worked Example

Here is a typical case. Both paths use the same cash: ₹40 lakh on day one, then about ₹1.04 lakh every month for 20 years.

Input Value
Flat price ₹1.6 crore
Your down payment ₹40 lakh (25%)
Home loan ₹1.2 crore at 8.5% for 20 years
Monthly EMI About ₹1,04,139
Maintenance 1% of the flat's value a year
Flat price growth 5% a year
SIP return 12% a year
Inflation 6% a year

The property path. Over 20 years you pay about ₹2.50 crore in EMIs, of which about ₹1.30 crore is interest. The flat grows to about ₹4.25 crore, which is only about ₹1.32 crore in today's money. And that is before maintenance and purchase costs.

The SIP path. The same ₹40 lakh and the same monthly amount go into a SIP instead. After tax and inflation, it ends up at roughly three times the flat's value in today's money. The exact figure depends on how returns are compounded.

That is a wide gap, but it belongs to these inputs. The 12% is an assumption, not a promise, and equity can fall for years. A lower SIP return, faster price growth for the flat, or a shorter period can narrow the gap, and sometimes flip it. That is why "SIP always wins" and "property always wins" are both wrong. The honest answer is: it depends on your numbers.

So Which One Wins?

It depends on four things you control or can estimate:

  • How fast property prices grow in your area (this varies a lot by city and even by neighbourhood)
  • How much you trust long-term equity returns
  • Your tax situation
  • How many years you are comparing

As a rough pattern:

  • The SIP looks stronger when property prices grow slowly compared with inflation, when the period is long, and when equity returns hold up.
  • Property closes the gap, and sometimes wins, when prices grow fast, when the period is short, or when you would otherwise pay rent. This article does not compare renting, since that is a different decision.

How to Remove Inflation

Divide every future amount by (1 + inflation) ^ years. For the flat in our example: ₹4.25 crore ÷ 1.06^20 = about ₹1.32 crore. Do the same for the money you put in, so that you compare profit with profit in today's money. Use the same inflation rate on both sides.

What the Calculator Includes and Leaves Out

Included: inflation adjustment on both sides, your down payment invested on the SIP side, capital gains tax applied once at the end, and yearly maintenance.

Not included: stamp duty and registration, broker fees, fund charges (treat your SIP return as already after charges), home-loan tax deductions, and rent. Leaving out purchase costs makes the property side look a little better than it is, so the real gap in favour of the SIP would be wider.

The Tax on Selling a Property, in Numbers

In our example, the flat bought for ₹1.6 crore sells for about ₹4.25 crore, a profit of about ₹2.65 crore. At 12.5%, the tax is roughly ₹33 lakh. That ignores some allowed deductions, so treat it as a rough size. If you put the profit into another house within the allowed time (Section 82, formerly Section 54), part or all of it can be exempt, up to a ₹10 crore limit.

How Equity Funds Are Taxed

Sell units within 12 months and the profit is taxed at 20%. Sell after 12 months and it is 12.5% on profit above ₹1.25 lakh a year. That ₹1.25 lakh is one limit shared across all your equity and equity-fund gains in the year.

Income-tax Act, 2025 (Income Tax Department, Ministry of Finance)

  • 12.5% tax on long-term gains from property, and the lower-tax choice for land or buildings bought before 23 July 2024: Section 197
  • Holding periods: more than 24 months for a house, more than 12 months for equity-fund units: Income Tax Department, Tax on long-term capital gains
  • Putting the profit into another house, with the ₹10 crore limit: Section 82
  • Equity-fund gains: Section 196 (short-term, 20%) and Section 198 (long-term, 12.5% above ₹1.25 lakh)

Reserve Bank of India

  • Loan-to-value limits for housing loans, with a 75% cap on loans above ₹75 lakh: Reserve Bank of India (Commercial Banks – Credit Facilities) Directions, 2025 (see the Housing Finance provisions)

Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI)

  • Limits on what a mutual fund can charge: the SEBI (Mutual Funds) Regulations, 2026, in force from 1 April 2026, available at sebi.gov.in under Legal, then Regulations
  • Each fund's Scheme Information Document states its exact charges and exit load.

Stamp duty and registration (Maharashtra)

  • Set by the Maharashtra Stamp Act, 1958. Current rates and ready reckoner values are published by the Inspector General of Registration and Controller of Stamps, Maharashtra, at igrmaharashtra.gov.in.
