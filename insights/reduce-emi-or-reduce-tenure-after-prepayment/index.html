Reduce EMI or Reduce Tenure After a Prepayment?

Published 12 October 2026 · 6 min read (plus an optional 1 min Advanced Read)

You've saved up a lump sum and paid part of your home loan. Then your lender asks one more question: should we reduce your EMI, or reduce your tenure? It sounds like a small form field. It decides how much interest you pay over the next ten or twenty years.

Below is one worked example, with the numbers, so you can see what each choice does. No rates are being forecast. The 8.5% is only an example to make the arithmetic concrete.

The Two Choices

A prepayment pays down the amount you owe. After that, the lender can do one of two things with your repayment plan.

  • Reduce the EMI. Your instalment gets smaller. The loan still ends on the original date.
  • Reduce the tenure. Your EMI stays the same. The loan ends sooner.

In both cases you've paid the same lump sum. What differs is what you keep: a smaller bill every month, or fewer months of bills.

One Example, Two Outcomes

Say you owe ₹50 lakh at 8.5% a year, with 20 years left. Your EMI is ₹43,391. After one year you pay ₹5 lakh towards the loan. The table shows what happens next.

No prepayment₹43,39120 years₹54.14 lakhNone
Reduce tenure₹43,39116 years₹38.10 lakh₹16.04 lakh
Reduce EMI₹38,96420 years₹49.04 lakh₹5.09 lakh

Example only. Rate fixed at 8.5% for the whole period, ₹5 lakh paid with the 12th EMI, no charges or fees, no tax effects.

The same ₹5 lakh saves ₹16.04 lakh of interest if you reduce the tenure, and ₹5.09 lakh if you reduce the EMI. Choosing the lower EMI costs you about ₹10.94 lakh in extra interest. In return you get ₹4,427 a month of breathing room.

Why the Gap Is So Big

Interest is charged on what you still owe. When the EMI stays at ₹43,391, the balance falls quickly, so each month's interest is smaller than it would have been. When the EMI drops to ₹38,964, you repay principal more slowly, so the balance stays higher for longer and keeps earning interest for the lender.

The lower EMI is not wrong. It does cost you something every month, for the whole of the remaining loan.

The Middle Path: Lower EMI, Pay the Old One

Here is a detail that is easy to miss. If you choose "reduce EMI" and then keep paying your old ₹43,391 anyway, you've done exactly what "reduce tenure" does. In our example the loan ends after 16 years with ₹38.10 lakh of interest in both cases.

So the lower EMI works as a safety net. You can pay less in a tight month, and pay the old amount when you can. It only helps if you actually pay the old amount, which is the hard part. A bill that is lower on paper tends to get spent.

Timing Matters as Much as the Choice

The same ₹5 lakh, with the EMI kept (reduce tenure), saves very different amounts depending on when you pay it.

Waiting five years costs you more than ₹5 lakh of the saving. Early prepayments do the most work, because they remove interest that would have been charged for the longest time. A lump sum paid in year 15 still helps, but it saves a fraction as much. That is why the choice at the start of a loan counts for more than the same choice near the end.

When Reducing the EMI Makes Sense

Reducing the tenure saves more interest, but money is not only a maths problem. The lower EMI can be the better answer when:

  • your income is uncertain, and a smaller fixed payment lets you sleep better;
  • your emergency fund is thin and the extra monthly room would stop you borrowing elsewhere;
  • you have another loan at a higher rate (a credit card or a personal loan), and the freed-up cash would clear it faster;
  • a big expense is coming, such as school fees or a medical cost, and you need the room now.

If none of these apply and you can comfortably pay the old EMI, reducing the tenure is the cheaper route. It is not an instruction. It is how the arithmetic falls.

Check Before You Prepay

  • Prepayment charges. Under the Reserve Bank of India's directions, lenders must not charge pre-payment charges on floating-rate loans given to individuals for non-business purposes. That holds whatever the source of the money, whether you pay part or all of the loan, and with no minimum lock-in. The directions apply to loans sanctioned or renewed on or after 1 January 2026, and they replace earlier RBI circulars that already barred these charges on floating-rate home loans. Fixed-rate loans can still carry a charge if your loan agreement says so. Read your sanction letter.
  • Which option your lender will apply. Tell the lender in writing whether you want the EMI or the tenure reduced, and ask for the revised repayment schedule. Check it against your own numbers.
  • Your cash buffer. Once the money is in the loan, you can't take it back out. Keep an emergency fund first. Our article on how big an emergency fund should be covers it.

The same choice also shows up when interest rates change. When a lender resets the rate for a whole class of floating-rate borrowers because the benchmark has risen, RBI says it should offer you either a higher EMI or more EMIs (or a mix), a switch to a fixed rate where the lender offers it, and the option to prepay in part or in full at any time during the remaining term.

What the Example Leaves Out

The example keeps the rate at 8.5% for 20 years. Real floating rates move, and when they do, either your EMI or your tenure changes. It leaves out processing fees, insurance and any tax benefit on the interest you pay. Prepaying cuts the interest, so any deduction linked to that interest shrinks too. We don't model tax here, so check the Income Tax Department's current rules for your own case.

It also ignores what else you could do with the ₹5 lakh. That question deserves its own article. The example only compares the two ways of using a prepayment once you've decided to make one.

The Bottom Line

Reduce the tenure and you save much more interest, because the balance falls faster. Reduce the EMI and you get monthly room at a price. The best of both is a lower EMI that you keep paying at the old amount whenever you can. Whichever you choose, pay early if you're going to pay, and tell your lender in writing.

How the New EMI Is Worked Out

When you choose "reduce EMI", the lender recalculates the instalment so the smaller balance is still repaid by the original end date. With balance B, monthly rate i (the yearly rate divided by 12) and n months left, EMI = B × i ÷ (1 − (1 + i)−n).

In the example, after twelve EMIs you owe ₹49,00,489. The ₹5 lakh prepayment takes that to ₹44,00,489, with 228 months left at 8.5% ÷ 12. That gives an EMI of ₹38,964, which matches the table. The old EMI was ₹43,391, so the monthly relief is ₹4,427.

Where the Saving Comes From

The saving is the interest you no longer pay on the money you've prepaid. In month 13, ₹5 lakh at 8.5% ÷ 12 is about ₹3,542 of interest that doesn't get charged. That's the first month's saving. With the EMI kept, that smaller interest bill means more of every EMI goes into principal, which lowers the next month's interest, and so on. The effect compounds in your favour, and it runs for as long as the loan has left to run. That is why an early prepayment saves so much more than a late one.

With the EMI reduced, you give most of that effect back. The smaller instalment repays principal more slowly, so the balance stays closer to what it would have been without the prepayment, and that balance keeps earning interest for the lender.
