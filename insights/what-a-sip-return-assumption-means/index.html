What a SIP Return Assumption Really Means

Published 12 October 2026 · 7 min read (plus an optional 2 min Advanced Read)

Every SIP calculator asks for an "expected return". You type in 12, press the button, and a big number appears. It's easy to forget that you made the 12 up.

This article is about that one box: what goes into it, what the calculator does with it, and why real life can end up far from the tidy answer on the screen. There are no fund names and no predictions here, only arithmetic you can check yourself.

It's a Number You Choose, Not a Number You're Given

A calculator doesn't know what your fund will earn. Nobody does. The rate you enter is an assumption, and every result on the page is only as sound as that assumption.

Behind the screen the sum is simple. The calculator divides your yearly rate by 12, applies that monthly rate to your balance, and adds your instalment at the end of each month. The same rate, every month, for the whole period. No market has ever behaved like that.

The Same SIP at Different Rates

Here is ₹10,000 a month for 20 years. In total you pay in ₹24 lakh. The rates below are examples to show the effect. They are not forecasts, and they are not what anyone should expect.

Assumed return per yearValue after 20 yearsSame value in today's rupees
6%₹46.2 lakh₹14.4 lakh
8%₹58.9 lakh₹18.4 lakh
10%₹75.9 lakh₹23.7 lakh
12%₹98.9 lakh₹30.8 lakh

Instalments at the end of each month, monthly compounding, before tax. The last column assumes inflation of 6% a year, which is also just an assumption.

Look at the last column. At a 6% return the pot only grows as fast as prices do. The ₹14.4 lakh in today's money is close to what you'd get by setting aside the same ₹10,000 a month at today's prices, which comes to about ₹14.1 lakh. You've kept pace. You haven't got ahead.

One Percentage Point Is Bigger Than It Looks

Take the 10% row. Now change the assumption to 9%. After 20 years the pot is ₹9.15 lakh smaller, roughly 12% less. Move it up to 11% instead and you add about ₹10.6 lakh.

You changed one digit in a number you invented, and the answer moved by lakhs. That is compounding at work over 240 months. It's also the reason to treat the calculator's single big figure as one point on a range, not as the answer.

An Average Return Is Not What You End Up With

Say you put ₹1,00,000 into something that gains 50% in the first year and loses 50% in the second. The average of +50 and −50 is zero, so you might expect your ₹1,00,000 back. You get ₹75,000. It goes up to ₹1,50,000 and then halves.

The figure that matches what actually happened is the compound annual growth rate, or CAGR. It is never higher than the simple average, and the more the returns jump around, the wider the gap. The steady rate you type into a calculator is meant to stand in for that compounded figure, whether the screen says so or not.

For a SIP, the Order of Good and Bad Years Matters

A lump sum only cares about the total change. A SIP cares about when the changes happen, because you keep adding money. Here are the same five yearly returns in two different orders, with ₹10,000 paid in every month for five years.

OrderReturns, year by yearPaid inValue after 5 years
Good years first+30, +20, +10, −10, −30₹6.00 lakh₹4.65 lakh
Bad years first−30, −10, +10, +20, +30₹6.00 lakh₹8.56 lakh

Illustration only. Each yearly return is spread evenly across the twelve months of that year.

Both rows contain exactly the same five numbers. The simple average is 4% a year in both, and the compounded rate is about 1.6% a year in both. A steady 1.6% would have given roughly ₹6.24 lakh. Yet one order leaves you with less than you paid in, and the other with about ₹2.5 lakh more than you paid in.

The reason is timing. In the first row the crash comes last, when you have put in the most money. In the second the crash comes first, when little is invested, and your later instalments are bought after prices have fallen. A calculator with one steady rate can't show you this, which is another reason to look at a range.

How to Pick a Number You Can Live With

  • Run three rates, not one. A cautious one, a middle one and a hopeful one. Look at the cautious result first.
  • Don't paste in a fund's last one, three or five year return. That describes a period that is over. Fund advertisements in India carry a warning that past performance may or may not be sustained in future and is not a guarantee of any future returns. It is there for a reason.
  • Keep it inside a sensible range. Under guidelines that AMFI framed with SEBI, a fund company's own SIP calculator lets you pick a return between 2% and 13% and never shows more than that. Your calculator doesn't have to follow that rule, but it is a useful check. If you find yourself typing 18 or 20, ask why.
  • Be more careful over short periods. The shorter the time, the wider the spread of outcomes can be. Over long periods results tend to even out, though nothing guarantees it.
  • Ask what happens if only the hopeful rate works. If your goal is met at the cautious rate, you have room. If it is met only at the hopeful one, the plan is fragile and worth rethinking.
  • Check the answer in today's rupees. A large number in 2046 will buy much less than it does now.

Then try it on your own plan. Open the SIP Growth tool, run it at three different rates, and compare all three results, not just the best one.

What a Basic Calculator Leaves Out

This section is about a plain SIP calculator, the kind that takes a rate and gives you one number. Some tools do more. Here is what to check in any of them.

Tax. Many basic calculators stop before tax, and every table in this article does too. Our SIP Growth tool does show tax, taken once when you sell. Tax comes when you sell, not while you invest. For units of an equity-oriented fund (one that keeps at least 65% of its money in shares of listed Indian companies), long-term gains above ₹1.25 lakh in a tax year are taxed at 12.5% under Section 198 of the Income-tax Act, 2025. Gains on units sold sooner are short-term and are taxed at 20% under Section 196. Both rules apply where securities transaction tax applies to the sale. Units count as long-term once you have held them for more than 12 months, and each monthly instalment starts its own clock, so one SIP can hold both kinds of units at once. The SIP Growth tool applies the long-term rate to the whole gain at the end, which is a simplification. Cess and surcharge may apply on top. Either way, an after-tax result will be lower than the before-tax tables here.

Costs. Reported returns of a mutual fund are already after the fund's running costs, so the rate you assume should be one you would actually receive, after costs.

You. A simple SIP calculator assumes you never skip, stop or change an instalment, and that you stay invested through every fall. People don't always manage that, so a calculator result is a best case for discipline as well as for returns.

The Bottom Line

A return assumption is a guess wearing a number. Use three of them, plan around the cautious one, and read any single result as one possible future rather than a forecast. The calculator can show you how sensitive your plan is. It can't tell you what the market will do.

The Maths Behind the SIP Value

With a monthly instalment P, a yearly rate R and n months, let i = R ÷ 12. With instalments at the end of each month, the value is P × ((1 + i)n − 1) ÷ i. For P = ₹10,000, R = 10% and n = 240 this gives ₹75,93,688, which matches adding the months up one by one.

There is a small catch. A "10% a year" rate applied as 10 ÷ 12 per month compounds to about 10.47% over a full year, so the calculator is a little more generous than a plain 10% a year. Different tools make different choices here, and the same inputs can give slightly different answers.

Average Against CAGR

For yearly returns r1 to rk, CAGR = ((1 + r1) × … × (1 + rk))1/k − 1. For the +50% and −50% example that is the square root of 0.75, minus 1, or about −13.4% a year. The gap between the simple average and CAGR is roughly half the variance of the returns. In the five-year example the average is 4%, and half the variance is about 2.3 points, which is close to the actual gap of 2.4 points (4% against 1.57%).

Measuring a Real SIP

Because each instalment goes in on a different date, the fair measure of a real SIP's return is a money-weighted rate such as XIRR, not the fund's CAGR over the period. Two people in the same fund can have different XIRRs if they invested on different dates.

What "Today's Rupees" Means

The last column of the first table divides the final amount by (1 + inflation)years, here 1.0620 = 3.21. Instalments paid later are in cheaper rupees, so the fair comparison for what you paid in is each instalment divided by the same factor for its own date, not the ₹24 lakh total. That is why the article compares the ₹14.4 lakh with ₹14.1 lakh, not with ₹24 lakh.
