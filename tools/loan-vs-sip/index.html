The question this answers

This compares two ways of using the same money when you already have a home to live in: buy a second property with a loan, or invest the same down payment and the same monthly EMI in a SIP. It does not compare renting with buying your own home. If you don't own a home yet, the answer can be very different, because a home you live in also saves you rent.

Today's rupees, on both sides

Every amount that is paid later is brought back to today's value by dividing by (1 + inflation) for each year that passes. That is done for the EMIs, the upkeep and the SIP instalments alike. If one side is discounted and the other isn't, the comparison tilts unfairly. The down payment is paid today, so it is not discounted.

The property path

The property grows at your assumed rate. When you sell, selling costs come off first. Tax is then charged on what is left above the price you paid. What remains is brought back to today's money. From that we subtract the down payment, the EMIs and the upkeep, also in today's money. Upkeep stays at the first year's rupee amount and does not rise.

The SIP path

The down payment is invested on day one. A monthly SIP equal to the EMI follows for the whole tenure, with each instalment at the end of its month. Both grow at the yearly return divided by 12, compounded monthly. Tax is worked out once, at the end: 12.5% of the gain above ₹1,25,000 (Section 198 of the Income-tax Act, 2025, which was Section 112A of the 1961 Act). This treats every rupee of gain as long-term.

Property tax

The Income Tax Department states long-term gains on land and buildings are taxed at 12.5% without indexation. Resident individuals who bought before 23 July 2024 can choose 20% with indexation instead. Exemptions such as reinvesting the gain, cess, surcharge and the holding period needed for a gain to count as long-term are not modelled. Check the Income Tax Department's capital gains page for your case.

Loan-to-value limit

RBI's housing finance rules limit how much a bank can lend against a property: up to 90% of the property value for loans up to ₹30 lakh, 80% for loans above ₹30 lakh and up to ₹75 lakh, and 75% for loans above ₹75 lakh. The page warns you if your numbers go above the limit for that loan size. Lenders may set stricter limits. Source: RBI Master Circular, Housing Finance.

What is not included

Rent you would pay or collect, tax benefits on the loan, stamp duty, registration, brokerage when buying, fund running costs, exit loads, and changes in the loan rate. Real results will differ. It is normal for either gain to come out negative.

How this comparison works

Comparing real estate bought with a home loan against equity mutual fund SIPs is one of the most common dilemmas for Indian households. A loan lets you control a large asset today with borrowed money, while a systematic investment plan (SIP) lets you build wealth steadily in liquid financial assets without debt.

To compare the two fairly, both paths must commit the exact same cash flows over time:

  • Path 1 (Property with loan): You pay the down payment upfront. Every month, you pay the loan EMI plus recurring maintenance costs. At the end of the loan tenure, the property is sold, deduction is made for selling expenses (brokerage and legal costs), and capital gains tax is paid.
  • Path 2 (SIP investment): The exact same down payment is invested as a lump sum on day one. Every month, the exact same amount that would have gone towards EMI plus maintenance is invested into an equity mutual fund SIP. At the end of the tenure, the units are redeemed and long-term capital gains (LTCG) tax is deducted.

Real gain in today's rupees

A crore twenty years from now will buy far less than a crore buys today. This calculator converts all future proceeds and monthly cash outflows into today's purchasing power using your chosen inflation rate:

Value in today's rupees = Future amount ÷ (1 + inflation)^years

The "real net gain" shows what was created above and beyond the purchasing power of the money you put into each option. This ensures that inflation is not treated as wealth.

Taxes and regulatory rules

Under the Income-tax Act, capital gains are taxed differently across property and mutual funds:

  • Mutual fund gains: Listed equity fund gains held for more than 12 months are subject to 12.5% LTCG tax on the gain exceeding ₹1,25,000 per financial year (Section 198 of the Income-tax Act, 2025, previously Section 112A).
  • Property gains: Long-term property capital gains are taxed based on the prevailing tax rate. For properties purchased after 23 July 2024, the rate is 12.5% without indexation benefit.
  • LTV caps: The Reserve Bank of India caps home loan borrowing to 90% for loans up to ₹30 lakh, 80% for loans between ₹30 lakh and ₹75 lakh, and 75% for loans above ₹75 lakh.

What this calculator leaves out

  • One-time acquisition costs: Stamp duty (typically 5% to 7% depending on state), registration fees, and legal verification charges on the property purchase.
  • Rental yield or saved rent: This calculator assumes the buyer already has a home to live in, comparing the property purely as an investment against an equity portfolio.
  • Fund management costs: Mutual fund total expense ratio (TER) and any applicable exit loads.
  • Interest rate resets: Floating home loan interest rates reset periodically with RBI repo rate changes.

Key trade-offs beyond the numbers

Liquidity: Mutual fund units can be redeemed in two working days. Selling physical real estate can take several months to a year, requiring extensive documentation and site visits.

Divisibility: You can redeem ₹50,000 from an SIP portfolio in an emergency without touching the rest. A property cannot be partially sold.

Debt obligation: An EMI is a fixed legal obligation. If household income drops, missed EMIs harm your credit score and risk loan default. SIPs can be paused or stopped at any time without penalty.
