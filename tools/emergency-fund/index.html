Where to keep your emergency fund in India

Tier 1: Instant Cash Reserve (1 Month Essentials)

Keep roughly 1 month of essential expenses in a high-yield savings bank account with instant ATM/UPI access or auto-sweep fixed deposit (FD). This pays for urgent hospital admissions, immediate car repairs, or home pipe bursts without waiting for market cut-offs.

Tier 2: Liquid Reserve (Remaining 5–11 Months)

Keep the rest across high-safety, low-volatility instruments:

  • Direct Liquid or Overnight Mutual Funds: Offer instant redemption up to ₹50,000 per folio or T+1 morning payout. Zero exit loads after 7 days.
  • Multi-bank Sweep FDs: Spreading across 2 distinct Scheduled Commercial Banks (ICICI, HDFC, SBI) keeps you within the ₹5,00,000 DICGC insurance limit per bank.
  • Never in equity or volatile debt: Never park emergency funds in equity funds, hybrid funds, or credit-risk debt funds to chase extra yield. Liquidity and capital safety trump returns.

The Emergency Fund: The Foundation of Wealth Creation

Before putting a single rupee into equity SIPs, real estate down payments, or stocks, an Indian household must build an untouchable emergency reserve. Without one, the first financial shock—a job loss, a family medical emergency, or a major auto repair—forces you to break long-term compounding by liquidating equity at market bottoms or taking predatory 18–36% personal loans.

How This Model Calibrates Your Target

A generic rule like "save 6 months of salary" is inefficient: it over-insures low spenders and under-insures high earners with heavy EMIs. This calculator models your actual non-negotiable burn rate and adjusts the target:

  • Variable or Freelance Income (+2 to +4 months): If your income depends on project billings or sales commissions, a prolonged drought requires a thicker cushion.
  • Single-Earner Household (+2 months): Having only one earning member carries 100% income concentration risk.
  • Dependants & Healthcare (+1 to +2 months): Non-salaried elderly parents or school-going children need liquidity for out-of-pocket medical bills that health policies may delay or partially reimburse.
