The EMI

With loan amount P, monthly rate r (the yearly rate divided by 12 and by 100) and n monthly payments, the EMI is P × r × (1 + r)n ÷ ((1 + r)n − 1). At a rate of 0% there is no interest and the EMI is simply P ÷ n.

Each month

Interest for the month is the balance you still owe multiplied by r. The rest of the EMI repays principal and lowers the balance. Next month's interest is charged on the smaller balance, so the interest part shrinks and the principal part grows. The yearly table adds up twelve of these months.

What it assumes

A fixed rate for the whole tenure, payments made on time at the end of each month, and no prepayments. All amounts are actual rupees in the future. No inflation adjustment is applied here. To see what a prepayment or a rate change does, use the Loan Prepayment Calculator.

What is not included

Processing fees, insurance, late charges, any prepayment charge your agreement may have, and tax benefits on the interest. Your lender's own schedule can differ slightly from this one because of rounding and how it counts days.

How an EMI is worked out

EMI stands for equated monthly instalment. It is the fixed amount you pay each month to clear both the interest and the principal of a loan over its tenure. Indian lenders use the reducing-balance method, which means interest each month is charged only on what you still owe.

EMI = P × r × (1 + r)n ÷ [(1 + r)n − 1]

P is the loan amount, r is the monthly rate (the yearly rate divided by 12 and by 100), and n is the number of months.

A worked example

Take a ₹20,00,000 loan at 7.5% a year for 20 years. The formula gives:

  • Monthly EMI: ₹16,112
  • Total interest over 20 years: ₹18,66,847
  • Total repaid: ₹38,66,847, almost double the amount borrowed. Interest on a long loan adds up because it is charged on the balance for many years.

Fixed and floating rates

This calculator keeps the rate the same for the whole loan. That fits a fixed-rate loan. Many home loans are floating, so the rate follows a benchmark and can move. When it does, either your EMI or your tenure changes.

When a lender resets rates for floating-rate borrowers, the Reserve Bank of India's FAQs say it should offer you a choice: a higher EMI or more EMIs (or a mix), a switch to a fixed rate where the lender offers one, and the option to prepay in part or in full. See the RBI FAQs on rate resets. The Loan Prepayment Calculator shows what a rate change does to your tenure and interest.

What this calculator leaves out

  • Processing fees. Lenders usually charge one when the loan is sanctioned. It is on top of the schedule here. Check your sanction letter.
  • Pre-payment charges. Under the RBI's Pre-payment Charges on Loans Directions, 2025, lenders must not charge pre-payment charges on floating-rate loans to individuals for non-business purposes, for loans sanctioned or renewed on or after 1 January 2026. A fixed-rate loan can still carry one if your agreement says so.
  • Loan insurance. If it is bundled with your loan, it adds to the real cost. Ask whether it is optional.
  • Tax benefits on the interest you pay. They depend on your own situation.

Common questions

Does a longer tenure always lower the EMI? At any rate above zero, yes, but you pay much more interest. Open the "other tenures" table in the full breakdown above and compare.

Can I lower my EMI after the loan has started? Prepaying part of the loan lets you ask your lender to reduce either the EMI or the tenure. The two choices save very different amounts of interest. Our article on reducing the EMI or the tenure after a prepayment explains why. Lender rules differ, so ask for the revised schedule in writing.

Why is so much of my early EMI interest? Interest is charged on the balance you still owe, and early on that balance has barely fallen. How your EMI splits between interest and principal follows one loan month by month.

Is the EMI the same at every bank for the same rate? The formula is the same, so yes. What differs between lenders is the rate, the fees and how they treat prepayments.
